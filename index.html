Model & controls

01

Crowd compression

Converging flows and constrained exits can create compression in otherwise ordinary crowds. Emergency survivors frequently cooperate; “mass panic” is a poor universal explanation.

HSE · Crowd hazards ↗Drury et al. · 2009 ↗
02

Bottleneck friction

At a crowded bottleneck, greater drive and friction can produce clogs. Running faster does not always cause this effect: geometry, density, and physical pushing matter.

Helbing, Farkas & Vicsek · 2000 ↗Pastor et al. · 2015 ↗
03

Barrier effects

A barrier can organize arrivals or move a bottleneck elsewhere. Human experiments do not establish that a pillar by an exit reliably improves evacuation.

Feliciani et al. · 2020 ↗Zuriguel et al. · 2020 ↗
04

Departure rates

Exit width, route availability, and staged departures all affect queues. Higher density can produce stop-and-go motion and involuntary displacement rather than free individual movement.

HSE · Crowd controls ↗Helbing et al. · 2007 ↗

Model definitions

Escaping compression: sustained or worsening contact can make a person abandon their exit arrow and seek a less crowded, visible patch of floor, including sideways or backward. Walls and bodies still block movement. Once contact and density ease, they briefly regroup and resume evacuation with stronger crowd avoidance and more willingness to wait. This caution fades gradually after a quiet period. Security does not hold people who are trying to escape compression. These are illustrative behavior rules.

Security: press S and click to place a guard. Guards watch visible traffic toward the exit and hold approaching people when congestion builds ahead. They release people as space opens, ease off if their queue compresses, and never hold one person for more than six seconds at a time. Blue shields mark guards; amber means demand exceeds local capacity. Each guard can hold up to 12 people at minimum panic, falling to 2 at maximum panic. Nearby guards share the load. These fixed control posts model instructions and braking, not solid barriers or real staffing ratios.

Exit arrows: drag from the sign toward its destination. The red area shows its 4.5 m visibility range, blocked by walls. About 85% follow on each encounter; others ignore it for 2–4 seconds. Equally crowded arrows have equal odds; less crowded directions get higher odds. Agents replan around walls, fallen people, and congestion. Stalled agents abandon a sign and retry an exit route.

Each person follows a route to a reachable exit. A desired-velocity force, short-range repulsion, compressive contact, and tangential friction determine motion. A spatial grid plans routes around your walls; exits have no artificial people-per-second quota.

Exit panic is a gameplay shorthand for desired speed and reduced personal space. Calmer people avoid dense routes more strongly and may wait briefly before joining a queue. Higher panic reduces this avoidance and waiting. Departure timing is a separate control. It does not represent a measured psychological state or imply that people inevitably behave selfishly.

Contact is a normalized, smoothed overlap-force proxy from 0–100%, not newtons, pascals, or the density × velocity-variance “crowd pressure” metric in the 2007 paper. Density counts people within a 1 m circle and divides by π m²; it is biased low near walls.

Casualties use invented exposure thresholds: sustained high contact accumulates a dose; a fall occurs at 9 dose units, injury at 18, and death at 36 under continued high contact. Dose decays when contact drops. After two seconds of low contact, lower density, and enough room, fallen people have a chance to stand. Injured people recover more slowly and walk at 55% speed; their injury remains. Downed and dead people remain obstacles. These thresholds are for exploring feedback, have no medical calibration, and do not estimate real injury or fatality risk.

The model omits body orientation, assistance, smoke, stairs, barrier failure, smoke-limited vision, and real human decision-making. Routes assume knowledge of exits. No numerical result here establishes a safe venue capacity or barrier design.
