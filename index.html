Model & controls

01

Anticipation

People compare paths ahead and slow for queues before touching. Contact forces can still push someone who is trying to stop.

Moussaïd et al. · 2011 ↗Karamouzas et al. · 2014 ↗
02

Individual decisions

Agents remember visible exit queues and resist frequent route changes. Nearby companions loosely stay together and can separate in a jam.

Bode et al. · 2015 ↗Moussaïd et al. · 2010 ↗
03

Urgency & cooperation

Urgency raises preferred speed and reduces headway. At 90%+, emergency shoving escalates: less yielding, tighter gaps and stronger forward force. Cooperation also controls helping casualties. Injury and compression rapidly increase personal stress.

Drury et al. · 2009 ↗Nicolas et al. · 2017 ↗
04

Compression & barriers

More drive can cause frictional clogs, but does not always slow evacuation. A barrier can change contact without improving exit flow.

Helbing et al. · 2007 ↗Zuriguel et al. · 2020 ↗

Model 2.2

Research review · 23 papers, assumptions & evidence ↗ · Benchmarks ↗

Behavior: local agent planning, individual traits, route memory and physical contact. It runs entirely in your browser. It is a research-informed hybrid, not a validated reproduction of the cited models or a trained neural network.

Exit urgency: higher values increase desired speed and commitment to an exit. At 90%+ this sandbox deliberately increases shoving, even for cooperative agents; this threshold is a game setting. Severe sustained compression can make them seek open space at any urgency; after relief they regroup and retain caution. Cooperation adjusts courtesy and attempts to aid nearby casualties. Injury, compression and visible casualties raise personal stress, reducing waiting, route comparison and helping. Helpers need low pressure and room to assist recovery; dead people receive only a brief check. People in groups controls nearby pairs and trios; they slow briefly for visible companions but do not wait forever.

Age variation: 0% uses adults; higher values add children and older adults (up to roughly 25% each). Age and individual variation affect size, walking pace, resistance to pushing and compression susceptibility. Hover over a person to see their age. The injury multipliers are illustrative, not medical estimates.

Departure pattern: everyone at once, exact staged releases, or a varied response with a tail of delays up to 40 seconds. The varied distribution is illustrative. Agents already occupy the venue before release.

Navigation: routes assume knowledge of the floor plan and exits. Individual door choices use visible queues and fading memory. Walls block visual steering and signs; dynamic body obstacles are shared model knowledge. Local perception considers up to 24 nearby visible bodies. People still use disk-shaped collision geometry.

Security: press S to place a control post. Guards meter traffic using visible downstream congestion, release compressed upstream queues, and hold no individual longer than six seconds. Blue shields mark guards; amber means capacity is exceeded. Capacity falls from 12 to 2 with urgency and is a game rule, not a staffing ratio. Guards issue instructions rather than forming solid barriers.

Arrows: drag toward a destination. The red region shows a 4.5 m wall-clipped range. About 85% follow per encounter; conflicting arrows favor the less crowded direction. Followers hold their direction through brief queues and continue toward the indicated exit. Compliance and range are game settings, not measured sign-detection rates.

Contact is a normalized overlap-force proxy, not physical pressure or the density × velocity-variance measure in the literature. Density counts people in a 1 m disk; it is biased low near walls.

Casualties: invented contact-exposure thresholds govern falling, injury and death. Fallen people may stand after sustained relief with enough space; injured walkers remain slower. These outcomes are not medically calibrated.

Smoke, stairs, body rotation, carrying casualties, unfamiliar-building search and barrier failure are omitted. Tests and synthetic benchmarks check behavior and regressions; they do not validate real venue safety or predict real casualties.
